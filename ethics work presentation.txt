a. Black hat hacker
Definition: Someone who violates computer security for personal gain or malicious intent, without authorization.
Objective: Financial gain, data theft, system disruption, or simply proving they can breach a system.
Case study: In 2021, Uganda’s banking sector was hit by coordinated unauthorized intrusions targeting mobile money platforms. Hackers exploited API vulnerabilities in MTN Mobile Money and Airtel Money systems, siphoning funds from customer accounts. These individuals had no authorization — purely malicious, purely for financial gain.

b. Cracker
Definition: A hacker who breaks into systems specifically to crack software protections, license keys, or security mechanisms.
Objective: To bypass software protections — often to pirate software or gain unauthorized access to premium features.
Case study: Ugandan software piracy is rampant. Crackers have broken the license protections on accounting software like QuickBooks and Sage, distributing cracked versions across Kampala’s Kikuubo trading hub. The objective wasn’t destruction — it was bypassing payment to use premium software for free.

c. Malicious insider
Definition: A current or former employee, contractor, or business partner who intentionally misuses authorized access to harm the organization.
Objective: Sabotage, data theft, financial fraud, or revenge — they already have the keys, so they’re the most dangerous.
Case study: In 2019, employees at Crane Bank (before its takeover) were implicated in fraudulently manipulating internal banking records, siphoning funds using their privileged system access. They already had legitimate credentials — that’s what made it devastating. DFCU Bank has also had internal fraud cases involving staff exploiting access rights.

d. Industrial spy
Definition: Someone who engages in corporate espionage — stealing trade secrets, IP, or proprietary data from competitors.
Objective: Gaining competitive advantage, either for themselves or on behalf of a rival company or nation-state.
Case study: Uganda Telecom vs MTN/Airtel — there have been documented cases of employees being poached and allegedly carrying proprietary customer data and pricing strategies to competitor firms. At the regional level, the Rwandan and Ugandan telecom rivalry has seen allegations of corporate intelligence gathering.

e. Hacktivist
Definition: A hacker motivated by political or social ideology rather than financial gain.
Objective: To protest, embarrass, or disrupt organizations they oppose — think Anonymous attacking government sites.
Case study: In 2016, during Uganda’s contested presidential election, a group calling themselves “Uganda Cyber Army” defaced government websites including NITA-U affiliated pages, protesting electoral fraud. Their objective wasn’t money — it was making a political statement about Museveni’s government.

f. Cyberterrorist
Definition: Someone who uses cyber attacks to intimidate or coerce governments or societies in pursuit of political/ideological goals.
Objective: Fear, mass disruption, or destruction of critical infrastructure — power grids, financial systems, hospitals.
Case study: While Uganda hasn’t had a confirmed large-scale cyberterror attack, the 2010 Kampala bombings by Al-Shabaab showed their capability for coordinated attacks on Ugandan soil. Security analysts have since flagged that Al-Shabaab and ADF rebels have been developing cyber capabilities to target Uganda’s critical infrastructure — UMEME (power grid) and Bank of Uganda have both been listed as high-risk targets in NITA-U threat assessments.
